Design audit · three Plotly figures · companion to the table audit

Charts that tell, not host.

The three charts are Plotly exports left at their default settings — a ridge plot of guest age, a 10-line gender‑over‑time graph, and a bubble plot of male share by age decade. The data is good, the encodings are reasonable, but the figures are doing what Plotly defaults always do: hosting the data instead of explaining it.

The fixes here are different from the table audit: tables suffer from too much chrome around precise numbers; charts suffer from summary statistics hidden in tooltips, spaghetti curves the eye can't separate, and encodings tuned for the data, not the story. Below: six cross-cutting habits, then three before / after redesigns.

A · Cross-cutting

Six chart habits worth dropping.

01

Stats hidden in hover

Median 48, IQR 42–75 is the most useful thing the ridge plot knows about each show — and it only appears when you hover. In a static export, it never shows up. Print the median and IQR directly on each row, in text.

02

Spaghetti at ten lines

Ten shows on a single line chart cross, overlap, and become a colourful blur. A category-coded legend at the side compounds the problem. Small multiples — one mini panel per show, with the overall trend ghosted behind for context.

03

Plotly chrome by default

The hover toolbar, "plotly" watermark, double tick marks, light blue gridlines, and the default Plotly typeface signal "this is a notebook export," not "this is finished work." Strip the modebar, set staticPlot: true, override fonts, drop the watermark — or render as SVG.

04

No baseline of reference

"60.6 % male" tells the reader very little until they know what 50 % looks like. A reference line at parity is essential — and so is a label saying so. Always draw the reference. Always label it inline.

05

Equal weight to outliers

The bubble plot shows "110–119 years, 100 % male" the same way it shows "50–59 years, 67.6 % male" — but the first bin holds 7 people and the second 1 565. Tiny-n bins shout as loudly as the meaningful ones. Encode n in size, then dim or drop bins under a threshold.

06

Y-axis runs 0–100

The gender chart axis starts at 0 even though no real value goes below 50 %. Half the canvas is empty and the actual variation is squeezed into a thin band at the top. For non-zero baseline ratios, crop to where the data lives — say 40–100 %.

07

Sample size invisible

StarTalk's age IQR runs 42–75 because the show has 11 unique guests. Markus Lanz's similar IQR is built from 5 234. The eye can't tell which is signal and which is noise. Always print n next to the show name. Where reasonable, fade rows where n is too small.

08

Pick the right encoding

A ridge plot is built to show shape. But if all the reader takes away is "median and spread per show," the shape is decoration. If only summary stats matter, draw the box, not the density.

Ridge plot of guest age — 01_age_ridge_plot_en.html

After
redesign · IQR strips with median

What changed

  • Encoding swap. A ridge plot is built to compare shapes; here, the reader only needs median & spread. So: each show becomes one horizontal IQR strip with a median tick. Less ink, more meaning.
  • Stats come out of hover. Median, Q1–Q3 and span are printed next to the row in mono — no interaction required.
  • Sorted by median, oldest at bottom. The eye now traces the age gradient in a single sweep — Lanz/Miosga skew young, Precht/Frühschoppen old.
  • Decade gridlines, in the lightest ink possible, replace the busy default Plotly grid.
  • Two annotations call out the extremes — the chart spends its space on the story, not the chrome.
  • Show colour is one 9 px dot, never the whole bar. Identity ≠ data.

After
redesign · small multiples

What changed

  • One line → ten panels. Ten lines on one axis blur together. Faceting gives each show its own pane and its own story.
  • Overall trend ghosted in every panel as the comparison baseline — the reader can answer "is this show above or below the median trajectory?" instantly.
  • 50 % parity line drawn into each panel, dashed and labelled the first time.
  • Y range cropped to 40–100 %; no data lives below 40, so showing 0–40 was empty canvas.
  • Latest value printed next to the line. No hover, no legend lookup.
  • Δ from first to last year appears under each show name — instant headline number per panel.
  • Show colour reduced to a dot and a single line stroke. No more ten overlapping fills.

Before

After
redesign · divergence dot plot

What changed

  • Centred on parity, not on zero. The x-axis becomes "points above / below 50 %" so the eye reads "how skewed is each decade?" not "how big is each number?"
  • One dot per row, sized by n. Bubble area still carries sample size, but the position now does the actual work — no more visually fighting two encodings on the same point.
  • Tiny-n bins faded out. The "100 % male, 110–119" bubble is still there but greyed — it can't shout louder than 50–59 (n=1 565) any more.
  • Connecting trace shows the arc of male over-representation peaking in the 70s and tapering on both sides.
  • Counts printed inline. n=1 565 means more than a 40 px bubble does on its own.
  • Parity baseline labelled, not just drawn.
Closing rules · charts

Six rules to apply across these and any future Plotly exports.

  1. Surface every number the chart's title implies. If the title says "Median age," show the median; don't hide it in hover.
  2. Pick the encoding that answers the question. Ridge for shape, dot-plot for ranking, small multiples for cross-group time trends, divergence for "how far from this benchmark?"
  3. Always draw the reference. 50 % parity, last year's value, the overall mean — whatever the reader is implicitly comparing against, render it.
  4. Weight by n. Either visually (size) or by exclusion (drop / fade small bins). Never let a 7-person decade speak louder than a 1 500-person one.
  5. Crop the axis to the data. Zero-baselines are a rule for bar charts; for percentages and ratios, show only the range that matters.
  6. Strip the Plotly defaults. No modebar, no watermark, no double tick marks. Either override the layout aggressively or render to SVG and never look back.
